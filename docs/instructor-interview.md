# Adaptive instructor interview

Use this guide during course onboarding or when a new module exposes a consequential gap. It is a question bank, not a form to read aloud.

## Interview principle

Inspect first, then ask the smallest useful question. Build on the instructor's language, acknowledge the answer briefly, and narrow the next question based on what changed.

Ask at most three questions in one message. Prefer a conversational sequence of short rounds over a long intake questionnaire.

## Opening round: find the north star

Useful questions include:

- “When students finish this course, what should they be able to do that they cannot do now?”
- “Where do students currently get stuck or form the wrong mental model?”
- “Should this platform mainly prepare students before class, support them during exercises, or help them revise afterwards? I can design for a mixture, but one primary role keeps it coherent.”

If the source material already answers one, do not ask it again. Reflect the emerging goal in one sentence and invite correction.

## Learner model

Ask only what affects design:

- What knowledge can be assumed reliably?
- Which terminology, mathematical maturity, coding experience, or professional context varies?
- Are there language or accessibility considerations that change examples or representation?

Do not ask for cohort demographics that are irrelevant to the learning experience.

## Target performance

Turn “understand X” into a choice the instructor can evaluate:

- explain or interpret;
- calculate or execute;
- implement;
- diagnose or critique;
- design or transfer to a new case.

Example: “Should students mainly interpret the output, calculate it by hand, or implement the method? I recommend interpretation plus one calculation if this module prepares them for the lab.”

## Teaching character

Ask what should feel recognisably like this instructor's course:

- preferred kinds of examples or analogies;
- degree of formality;
- whether to lead with intuition, formalism, or a real problem;
- feedback tone;
- notational or visual conventions that must remain consistent.

Inspect representative slides, diagrams, and handouts before asking about visual direction. When the cues are coherent, propose a short material-grounded direction for correction instead of asking the instructor to design a website. Ask which cues should remain recognisable only when the sources conflict or the answer changes institutional branding, typography, imagery, or formality. Confirm rights before using a logo, font, photograph, illustration, or recurring motif.

These answers belong in `course/MEMORY.md` once confirmed.

When interaction will be central, ask what learners should physically or mentally do in the course: predict, manipulate, trace, construct, diagnose, compare, or explain. Ask whether visible progression and light celebration feel motivating or distracting for this cohort. Do not ask the instructor to choose UI widgets.

## Constraints and publication

Confirm only when relevant:

- formative versus graded use;
- whether hints or full solutions are appropriate;
- which diagrams, readings, or questions may be reproduced;
- release timing, login, analytics, or external-service constraints.

Never infer publication rights from a file being present in `materials/`.

## Close each round well

After answers:

1. summarize the updated interpretation in one to three bullets;
2. say what you will record in memory;
3. surface any remaining decision that blocks responsible work;
4. otherwise proceed autonomously to the agreed artifact.

Do not repeatedly ask “Is that okay?” for reversible implementation choices. Show a concrete result that the instructor can react to.

## Signals to stop interviewing

Stop asking and begin work when the source boundary, learner, target performance, assessment stakes, and publication constraints are sufficiently clear for the current task. Unknown information that does not change the next action should remain a labelled assumption or open question.
